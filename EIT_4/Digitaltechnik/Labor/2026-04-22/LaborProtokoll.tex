\begin{abstract}
This report documents the experimental investigation of monostable and astable
multivibrators, conducted in Digi Lab~1 at Fachhochschule Vorarlberg on
21/22~April~2026.
Three circuits were assembled and analysed: a monostable multivibrator based on the
NE555 timer IC, an astable multivibrator also using the NE555, and an astable
oscillator built from two CMOS inverters (74C00).
Calculated values for pulse duration and oscillation period were compared against
oscilloscope measurements. The monostable circuit showed a deviation of 7.3\%, 
increasing to 8.2\% with a second IC. Direct BNC measurements proved that 
low-impedance connections disrupt the circuit, preventing the output from returning 
to low. The astable NE555 circuit oscillated at 495.8\,Hz, deviating 3.1\% from 
theory. Results confirmed that component tolerances and probe loading are the 
primary causes of error. Observations also showed that bypass capacitors are 
essential for stable switching edges. All circuits performed according to 
theoretical models within acceptable margins.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Switching circuits are fundamental building blocks in digital and mixed-signal
electronics. This laboratory exercise focuses on two important classes of such
circuits: multivibrators, which generate or shape rectangular pulse signals, and
oscillators built from logic gates.

A \textbf{monostable multivibrator} (one-shot) produces a single output pulse of
defined duration in response to a trigger event. An \textbf{astable multivibrator}
oscillates continuously between two states without any external trigger, generating
a periodic square wave. Both types can be realised with the NE555 timer IC, whose
internal structure consists of two comparators, a flip-flop, a discharge transistor,
and an output stage.

The \textbf{NE555} is a versatile and widely used timer IC. In monostable mode, the
output pulse width is determined solely by an external RC network according to
$t_H = 1.1\,R\,C$. In astable mode, the frequency and duty cycle depend on two
resistors and one capacitor.

As a complementary approach, an astable oscillator can also be constructed from two
cascaded CMOS inverter gates with a feedback RC network.

The objectives of this laboratory are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item To understand and verify the operating principle of monostable and astable
        multivibrators using the NE555 IC.
  \item To compare calculated and measured values for pulse duration and oscillation
        period, and to quantify and explain deviations.
  \item To investigate the influence of the measurement setup (oscilloscope probes
        vs.\ BNC cables) on the circuit behaviour.
  \item To analyse the astable multivibrator realised with two CMOS inverters.
\end{itemize}

The theoretical background was reviewed according to~\cite{Floyd2006}
and~\cite{TietzeSchenk1999}.

\section{Theoretical Background}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{NE555 Internal Structure}

The NE555 contains three equal resistors forming a voltage divider that sets the
reference levels at $\tfrac{1}{3}V_{CC}$ and $\tfrac{2}{3}V_{CC}$, two comparators,
an SR flip-flop, an npn discharge transistor (pin~7), and a push-pull output stage
(pin~3).

\subsection{Monostable Mode}

In monostable mode, the capacitor $C$ charges through $R_1$ after a falling trigger
edge at pin~2 pulls the output high. The output returns low once $V_C$ reaches
$\tfrac{2}{3}V_{CC}$, which gives the pulse duration:
\begin{equation}
  t_H = 1.1 \cdot R_1 \cdot C
  \label{eq:mono_theory}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Astable Mode}

In astable mode, $C$ charges through $R_1 + R_2$ and discharges through $R_2$ alone.
The resulting high and low times are:
\begin{align}
  t_H &= 0.693\,(R_1 + R_2)\,C \label{eq:astable_th_theory}\\
  t_L &= 0.693\,R_2\,C         \label{eq:astable_tl_theory}\\
  T   &= 0.693\,(R_1 + 2R_2)\,C \label{eq:astable_T_theory}
\end{align}

\subsection{RC Oscillator with CMOS Inverters}

Two cascaded inverters with a series resistor $R$ and capacitor $C$ in the feedback
path form a relaxation oscillator. The threshold voltages of the CMOS gates determine
the switching points, giving an oscillation frequency of approximately:
\begin{equation}
  f \approx \frac{1}{2.2\,R\,C}
  \label{eq:inv_osc_theory}
\end{equation}

% ── 3.1.3 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Task 3.1.3 -- Explanation of Differences}

The measured pulse durations of \SI{1.18}{\second} and \SI{1.19}{\second} deviate
from the calculated value of \SI{1.1}{\second} by approximately \SI{7.3}{\percent}
and \SI{8.2}{\percent} respectively.
The measured capacitor peak voltage of \SI{6.8}{\volt} is slightly above the
theoretical threshold of $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \SI{10}{\volt} = \SI{6.67}{\volt}$.

The primary cause of the deviation is the \textbf{capacitor tolerance of $\pm20\,\%$},
which alone is sufficient to explain the observed discrepancy.
Additionally, the 10:1 probe introduces a parasitic capacitance of approximately
\SI{10}{\pico\farad} in parallel with the timing capacitor, which slightly increases
the effective capacitance and therefore the pulse duration.
The NE555 internal comparator threshold also deviates slightly from the ideal
$\tfrac{2}{3}V_{CC}$, contributing a small systematic offset.

% ── 3.1.4 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Task 3.1.4 -- Measurement with BNC Cables}

The measurement from task~3.1.2 was repeated, replacing the 10:1 oscilloscope probes
with direct BNC cables.

A BNC cable connected to the oscilloscope presents an input impedance of
\SI{1}{\mega\ohm}, which is placed \textbf{in parallel with the timing capacitor}.
Together with the series resistance $R_1 = \SI{1}{\mega\ohm}$ of the circuit, this
forms a voltage divider with a ratio of:
\[
  \frac{R_{\text{BNC}}}{R_1 + R_{\text{BNC}}}
  = \frac{\SI{1}{\mega\ohm}}{\SI{1}{\mega\ohm} + \SI{1}{\mega\ohm}} = 0.5
\]

As a result, the maximum voltage across the capacitor is limited to approximately
$0.5 \times \SI{10}{\volt} = \SI{5}{\volt}$, which never reaches the NE555 threshold
of $\tfrac{2}{3}V_{CC} = \SI{6.67}{\volt}$.
Consequently, the comparator is never triggered and the output \textbf{never returns
to low} -- the monostable behaviour is completely disrupted.

In contrast, the 10:1 probe provides \SI{10}{\mega\ohm} input impedance, which is
negligible compared to $R_1 = \SI{1}{\mega\ohm}$ and does not significantly alter
the circuit behaviour.

% ── 3.2.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Task 3.2.1 -- Calculation of Oscillation Period}

Applying equations~\eqref{eq:astable_th_theory}--\eqref{eq:astable_T_theory}:
\begin{align*}
  t_H &= 0.693 \times (\SI{10}{\kilo\ohm} + \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}) \times \SI{100}{\nano\farad}
       = \SI{1.386}{\milli\second} \\
  t_L &= 0.693 \times \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm} \times \SI{100}{\nano\farad}
       = \SI{0.693}{\milli\second} \\
  T   &= t_H + t_L = \SI{2.079}{\milli\second} \\
  f   &= \frac{1}{T} = \SI{481.0}{\hertz} \\
  D   &= \frac{t_H}{T} = \frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1+2R_2} = \SI{66.7}{\percent}
\end{align*}
\[
  \boxed{T_{\text{calc}} = \SI{2.079}{\milli\second}, \quad
         f_{\text{calc}} = \SI{481}{\hertz}, \quad
         D_{\text{calc}} = \SI{66.7}{\percent}}
\]

\subsubsection{Task 3.2.3 -- Explanation of Differences}

The measured period of \SI{2.017}{\milli\second} deviates from the calculated
\SI{2.079}{\milli\second} by approximately \SI{3.0}{\percent}.
This deviation is well within the expected component tolerances of $\pm5\,\%$ for
resistors and $\pm20\,\%$ for capacitors and can therefore be attributed primarily to
\textbf{component tolerances}.

Compared to task~3.1, the influence of the oscilloscope probe on the measurement is
significantly smaller.
The probe's \SI{10}{\mega\ohm} input impedance is negligible relative to the circuit
resistances of only \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}, whereas in task~3.1 the probe impedance was
of the same order of magnitude as $R_1 = \SI{1}{\mega\ohm}$.
Consequently, probe loading has a much smaller effect here.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Messaufbau Schaltung Task 3.2.4.jpeg}
  \caption{Measuring setup}
  \label{fig:MeasuringSetup}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Task3.2.4.png}
  \caption{Zoomed rising edge of the NE555 astable output signal without a bypass (decoupling) capacitor.}
  \label{fig:astable_edge_without_bypass_Cap}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Task3.2.4 (2).png}
  \caption{Zoomed rising edge of the NE555 astable output signal with a bypass (decoupling) capacitor.}
  \label{fig:astable_edge_with_bypass_Cap}
\end{figure}

The rising edge of the NE555 output does not exhibit a perfectly steep transition.
This is caused by the output stage of the NE555, which consists of a
\textbf{BJT totem-pole structure}. When the output transistor switches from saturation
to cut-off, carrier storage time and the capacitive load at pin~3 (wiring, probe)
slow down the transition.

Furthermore, a \textbf{bypass (decoupling) capacitor} must be placed between $V_{CC}$
and GND as close as possible to the IC supply pins.
When the NE555 output switches, the IC momentarily draws a large transient current.
Without a bypass capacitor, this current must be supplied from the power supply through
the full inductance of the supply wires, causing a voltage dip at $V_{CC}$ and
potential instability of the switching threshold.
The bypass capacitor acts as a local energy reservoir that immediately provides the
required transient current, ensuring stable and reliable switching behaviour.

\]

% ── 3.3.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Task 1.1 -- Circuit Function}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Messaufbau Schaltung Task 3.3.1.jpeg}
  \caption{Measuring setup}
  \label{fig:MeasuringSetup}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Task3.3.1.png}
  \caption{Voltage at node P (C2 - red trace) and output $U_{\text{OUT}}$ (C1 - yellow trace)
           -- two-inverter oscillator.}
  \label{fig:inv_function}
\end{figure}

The circuit operates as a relaxation oscillator. The first inverter output feeds back
through the RC network ($R$ and $C$) to its own input at node~S.
When $U_{\text{OUT}}$ switches to a high level, the capacitor $C$ begins charging
through $R$, causing the voltage at node~S to rise exponentially.
Once $V_S$ crosses the CMOS switching threshold (approximately $\tfrac{1}{2}V_{CC}$),
the first inverter switches its output low, which in turn causes $U_{\text{OUT}}$ to
go high via the second inverter.
The capacitor then discharges in the opposite direction, and the cycle repeats,
producing a continuous square wave at the output.

\subsubsection{Task 1.3 -- Purpose of $R_S$}

Without $R_S$, the voltage at node~P would exceed the supply rails at every switching
event. The resulting current would flow directly through the CMOS input protection
diodes without any current limitation, potentially \textbf{destroying the IC}.

Additionally, $R_S$ forms part of the total series resistance in the RC network and
therefore has a small influence on the oscillation frequency.

\section{Summary}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This laboratory exercise verified the timing characteristics of NE555 and CMOS-based switching circuits. 
Measurements of the monostable multivibrator yielded a pulse duration of 1.18\,s, while the astable configuration produced a frequency of 495.8\,Hz. 
These results deviate from theoretical predictions by 7.3\% and 3.1\%, respectively, primarily due to capacitor tolerances and probe loading. 
The experiments demonstrated that high-impedance nodes require 10:1 probes to maintain circuit functionality. 
Ultimately, the data confirms that RC timing models accurately predict behavior when considering practical measurement effects and proper decoupling.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Outlook}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Future experiments could investigate methods to achieve a 50\% duty cycle in the NE555 astable mode by placing a signal diode in parallel with $R_2$. 
This modification allows the capacitor to charge only through $R_1$, enabling independent control of high and low times. 
Another relevant study involves testing the temperature dependence of the RC-based oscillators to quantify frequency drift under thermal stress. 
Additionally, varying the bypass capacitor values would help determine the minimum capacitance required to maintain switching stability across different frequency ranges.
